Who pays whom? Inside TripSplit's settlement algorithm

From building TripSplit, a full-stack expense-splitting app (React + ASP.NET Core)

Splitting a dinner bill is easy. Splitting a five-day trip — where different people paid for different things, and not everyone joined every expense — is where group chats go to die. TripSplit's job is to answer one question at the end of a trip: who should transfer how much to whom, in as few payments as possible?

Step 1: reduce everything to one number per person

Every expense has a payer, an amount, and a set of participants. Walk the ledger once: credit the payer the full amount, debit each participant an equal share. Each person ends with a single net balance — positive means the group owes them, negative means they owe the group. 

foreach (var e in expenses)
{
    var share = e.Amount / e.Participants.Count;
    balances[e.PayerId] += e.Amount;
    foreach (var p in e.Participants)
        balances[p.Id] -= share;
}

A useful property falls out of this: the balances always sum to zero. Every dollar credited to a payer is debited from participants. That invariant becomes the first unit test — if it ever breaks, the ledger logic is wrong.

Step 2: match debtors to creditors, greedily

Now the history doesn't matter anymore — only the net positions do. Suppose a trip ends like this:

Person Net balance
Alex +60 (gets back)
Bee −30 (owes)
Chen −30 (owes)

The naive answer — everyone pays back everyone they ever shared an expense with — explodes into a mess of tiny transfers. Instead, sort creditors and debtors by size and repeatedly match the largest debtor with the largest creditor, transferring the smaller of the two amounts: 

while (i < debtors.Count && j < creditors.Count)
{
    var pay = Math.Min(debtors[i].Amount, creditors[j].Amount);
    result.Add(new Settlement(debtors[i].Id, creditors[j].Id, pay));

    debtors[i].Amount   -= pay;
    creditors[j].Amount -= pay;

    if (debtors[i].Amount   < Epsilon) i++;
    if (creditors[j].Amount < Epsilon) j++;
}

Each iteration fully clears at least one person — the debtor, the creditor, or both. With n people holding non-zero balances, that bounds the plan at n − 1 transfers. For Alex, Bee and Chen: two payments of 30 to Alex, done.

Worth knowing: this greedy plan is not always the theoretical minimum number of transfers — finding that is NP-hard (it hides a subset-sum problem). But n−1 is a tight, predictable ceiling, the algorithm is O(n log n), and for real trip sizes the result is effectively optimal. A good trade-off beats a perfect one you can't compute.

Step 3: respect the cents

Divide 100 by 3 and someone's share is 33.333… Floating-point drift and rounding can leave people with balances like −0.0033, and a naive loop would happily generate a transfer of $0.00 to fix it. Two decisions handle this:

First, money is decimal, never double — C#'s decimal type is base-10 and doesn't accumulate binary representation error. Second, an epsilon of half a cent (0.005m) defines "settled": any residual smaller than that is noise, not debt. One unit test pins this down with the 100/3 case — three people, one expense, exactly two transfers of 33.33, nobody chased for a phantom cent.

Why this lives in its own service

In the codebase, all of this sits in a SettlementService with no database, no HTTP, no framework dependencies — pure input to output. The controller fetches the trip, hands people and expenses to the service, and returns the plan. That separation isn't ceremony: it's what makes the algorithm trivially unit-testable, and it means the same logic could back a CLI, a mobile app, or a batch job without touching a line of it.

The full implementation and its test suite are in the TripSplit repo.
